The Nature of Communication
Source: Burgoon & Ruffner (1979); Gamble & Gamble (2013); Locker et al (2024)
Definition

Communication is a process by which we assign and convey meaning in an attempt to create shared understanding. It is a two-way process of sending and receiving information, ideas, thoughts, feelings and goals — the sender can equally become the receiver in a constant stream of role interchange (Gamble & Gamble, 2013).

Communication is one of the most complex human activities to understand and define. It has an all-pervasive and ubiquitous influence on almost every other human endeavour. Dance and Larson (1974) identified over 91 interdisciplinary definitions of communication — spanning sociology, psychology, anthropology, politics, business, technology, philosophy, and more.

Two Key Elements in Any Definition

Element 1
Transmission
Something is transmitted (facts, feelings, ideas). There must be a receiver. The sender must consider the receiver when structuring and delivering the message — neglecting the receiver results in no response or a wrong response.
Element 2
Understanding
The correct interpretation of the message is vital. Shared understanding occurs only when the receiver understands the message in the same sense the sender intended. The greater the understanding, the more human action aligns with organisational goals.

The Communication Process

According to Locker et al (2024), every communication encounter — even in business — follows a consistent process:

Process Flow

Sender → (has a message) → Channel → Receiver

The process requires a vast repertoire of skills: intrapersonal and interpersonal processing, listening, observing, speaking, questioning, analyzing, and evaluating. It is through communication that collaboration and cooperation occur.

Dimensions of Communication in Business
Source: DeVito (2014); Gamble & Gamble (2013)

In business, communication is essential across all levels — internally within the organisation and externally with stakeholders, including people from different countries and cultural backgrounds.

1Formal vs. Informal Communication
Formal: Follows established hierarchy — memos, reports, official emails, policy manuals, formal meetings. Ensures clarity, accuracy, and accountability (DeVito, 2014).

Informal: Arises from social interactions — grapevine/rumors, casual chats, water cooler conversations. Fosters camaraderie but can spread misinformation.
2Vertical vs. Horizontal Communication
Vertical: Flows up and down the hierarchy.
• Downward — management to employees (instructions, policies, feedback)
• Upward — employees to management (suggestions, feedback, reports)

Horizontal: Between individuals/departments at the same level — essential for coordination, teamwork, and problem-solving.
3Oral vs. Written Communication
Oral: Face-to-face conversations, telephone calls, presentations, meetings. Allows immediate feedback but lacks a permanent record.

Written: Emails, memos, reports, proposals, letters, instant messaging. Provides a permanent record and allows careful consideration.
4Verbal vs. Nonverbal Communication
Verbal: Actual words used in a message — interpersonal, group, or public contexts.

Nonverbal: Body language (gestures, posture), facial expressions, touch (haptics), tone of voice (paralanguage), eye contact (oculesics), physical proximity (proxemics), timing (chronemics). Nonverbal cues often convey emotions and attitudes that words alone cannot.
5Internal vs. External Communication
Internal: Within the organisation — fosters employee engagement, knowledge sharing, organisational culture, improved processes.

External: With customers, suppliers, investors, media, and the public. Crucial for building and maintaining organisational reputation.
6Technological Communication
Email, instant messaging, video conferencing, social media, company intranets, and collaboration tools. Provides speed, efficiency, and global reach — but presents challenges like information overload, security risks, and the need for digital literacy.

Writing in Business: Proposals, Letters & Reports
Source: Argenti (2009); Lesikar et al (1999)

Writing is a key foundation on which business principle is built. Most communicative actions — letters, memos, reports, proposals, backgrounders, and position papers — require appreciable expertise in writing.

The Five Steps of the Writing Process

1Prewriting
Planning, research, outlining, diagramming, storyboarding, or clustering/mind mapping.
2Draft
Initial composition in prose form.
3Revision
Review, modification, and organization (by the writer).
4Editing
Proofreading for clarity, conventions, and style (preferably by another writer).
5Submittal
Sharing the writing through performance, printing, or distribution.
Note

These steps are not set in stone. Some may be merged or skipped depending on the project. Prewriting skills can be applied throughout the entire process.

Business Letters

Business letters remain a viable avenue for making enquiries, raising queries, and filing complaints. They serve as a structured and documented way to exchange ideas between organizations, employees, clients, or stakeholders (Argenti, 2009).

Purposes of Business Letters

  • Formal Communication: Job applications, formal inquiries, complaints, proposals, legal notices
  • Building Relationships: Demonstrates professionalism and attention to detail
  • Creating a Record: Documented trail for legal or contractual purposes
  • Conveying Important Information: Relaying critical information clearly and concisely

Structure of a Business Letter (Argenti, 2009)

Business Proposals

Definition

A business proposal is a formal, written document designed to persuade a prospective client, customer, investor, or partner that your product, service, or solution is the best way to address their specific needs or problems. By its nature, proposals are persuasive.

Two Types of Business Proposals (Lesikar et al, 1999)

Type 1
Solicited Proposal
More detailed. Responds to a published Request for Proposal (RFP) or Invitation for Bid (IFB). Meets specific requirements set by the requesting party. The winning bidder's proposal addresses the exact needs stated.
Type 2
Unsolicited Proposal
Like collateral marketing material. Broad spectrum — doesn't address a particular company or close a sale. Distributed via direct mail, left after cold calls, or at trade shows. Introduces a product or service to potential customers (Wagner, 1994).

Structure of a Business Proposal

  • Problem/Need Identification: Clearly articulate the prospect's problem or opportunity
  • Proposed Solution: Detail your product, service, or approach — specific, clear, and relevant
  • Benefits and Value Proposition: Tangible and intangible advantages (efficiency, cost savings, revenue, reputation)
  • Methodology and Implementation: Timelines, processes, and resources involved
  • Pricing and Terms: Cost, payment terms, and financial information
  • Qualifications and Experience: Company expertise, track record, team capabilities
  • Call to Action: Clear next steps (sign contract, schedule a meeting, approve the proposal)

Business Report Writing

Reports involve creating structured documents that provide information, analysis, or recommendations to assist in decision-making. A well-crafted report is clear, concise, and tailored to its audience.

Types of Business Reports

Type 1
Informational
Present factual data without significant analysis (e.g., sales reports, inventory reports).
Type 2
Analytical
Analyse data, interpret findings, and draw conclusions (e.g., market research, financial analysis).
Type 3
Recommendation
Analyse a problem and propose specific actions or solutions (e.g., feasibility studies, improvement proposals).
Type 4
Progress
Provide updates on the status of a project or initiative.
Type 5
Feasibility
Evaluate the viability of a proposed project or venture.

Structure of a Business Report

1Title PageReport title, author(s), date, recipient(s). Enables easy retrieval from storage.
2Executive SummaryBrief overview (350–550 words): objectives, main findings, conclusions/recommendations. Often the most-read section.
3Table of ContentsMain sections and subsections with page numbers.
4IntroductionBackground information, purpose, scope, and methodology overview.
5MethodologyMethods and procedures used to gather and analyse data.

Presentation of a Business Model
Source: Lucas & Stob (2023)

Effectively delivering speeches in public spaces is one of the most elegant communication skills an individual can have. Within business, presentations are used to introduce new products, compete for business opportunities, apply for grants, or stand for elected positions.

Four Styles of Presentation (Lucas & Stob, 2023)

1Manuscript Reading
Reading an entire speech from a prepared script. Effective for presentations involving key statistics that cannot be missed.
2Rote Memorisation
Relies on the excellent memory of the presenter who commits a whole script to memory.
3Extemporaneous Delivery
The most preferred by accomplished presenters. Uses a small note or PowerPoint to aid memory and guide the presentation — flexible and natural.
4Impromptu Delivery
When the presenter is called to speak with little or no notice. Relies entirely on existing knowledge and composure.

Format of a Presentation

Introduction

  • Start with a welcome message — creates familiarity and reduces anxiety
  • State early and clearly what the presentation is about: aims and objectives
  • Catch attention with a provocative statement or humorous anecdote
  • Example: "Genetically-modified crops could save millions from starvation"

Body (Middle)

  • Outline your argument or story in detail
  • In five minutes: only two or three main points — everything else supports these
  • List main headings and key phrases — do not overload with content
  • Use graphics or anecdotes to add variety — visuals may be the only take-away for many audience members

Conclusion

  • Briefly summarise your main points
  • Recap key points you want the audience to remember and act on
  • Respond to queries and clarifications
  • Thank the audience for active listening
  • End on a strong or positive note — smile, slow down, look at the audience
  • Example: "The benefits of organic farming are mind-blowing — so do not wait a second more to adopt it!"

Negotiation Skills in Business
Source: Locker et al (2024)
Definition

Negotiation skills are the abilities and techniques used by individuals or teams to reach mutually acceptable agreements with other parties — whether clients, suppliers, partners, employees, or internal stakeholders.

Negotiation occurs in business, non-profit organisations, government, legal proceedings, among nations, and in personal situations (employment salary, marriage, divorce, everyday life). It is an essential life skill for entrepreneurs.

Importance of Negotiation Skills

Securing Favorable Deals
Obtain better prices, terms, and conditions — leading to increased profitability and cost savings.
Building Strong Relationships
Foster trust and rapport, leading to long-term mutually beneficial relationships. Especially important in Nigeria where personal connections play a significant role.
Conflict Resolution
Provides a constructive way to resolve disputes, minimizing damage to relationships and avoiding costly litigation.
Win-Win Outcomes
Good negotiation aims to satisfy the needs of all parties, creating sustainable agreements.
Career Advancement
Strong negotiation skills are crucial for securing better job offers, salary packages, and promotions.
Strategic Advantage
In a competitive market, effective negotiation provides a significant edge over competitors.

Negotiation Strategies & Tactics

  • Making the first offer: Anchors the negotiation and sets the initial range
  • Focusing on interests, not positions: Understanding underlying needs leads to more creative solutions
  • Creating value: Expanding the "pie" so all parties benefit more
  • Trading concessions: Offering something of lesser value in exchange for something of greater value
  • Using objective criteria: Referencing market data, industry standards, or legal precedents
  • Building rapport: Establishing a positive connection for a more collaborative environment
  • Knowing your walk-away point: Understanding your absolute minimum prevents agreeing to a bad deal

Meaning of Business Model
Topic: Business Model Development
Definition

A business model is an organisation's system of transforming inputs, through its business activities, into outputs and outcomes that are aimed at fulfilling the organisation's strategic purpose and creating value over the short, medium and long term. It describes the rationale of how an organisation creates, delivers, and captures value.

The term "business model" was rarely used before the 1990s. The advent of the personal computer and the growing popularity of spreadsheets made it possible for people to strategically design businesses before launching. A business model is like a roadmap that helps entrepreneurs understand how the pieces of their business fit together — what they are doing, how, for whom, and why.

Key Insight

Business models are relevant to both startup and existing businesses. For startups, it tests feasibility of a new opportunity. For existing businesses, it articulates current operations and identifies new spaces for innovation or growth. Business models are temporary, subject to change, adjustable, and flexible.

7
The Four Parts of a Business Model

A business model consists of four main interlocking parts that together create "the business." No business can survive without all four parts. Competitive advantage can come from any of these parts — not just the product or service offered.

1The Offering
Identifies what you are offering to a particular customer segment, the value generated for those customers, and how you will reach and communicate with them. Includes the Customer Value Proposition (CVP) — how your products and services help customers do something more inexpensively, easily, effectively, or quickly than before.
2The Customers
People or businesses willing to pay for what you are offering. You must choose the customers you most want to target, determine how to reach them, and figure out how to build and maintain a relationship with them.
3The Infrastructure
All the key resources (people, technology, products, suppliers, partners, facilities) that an entrepreneur must have to drive the value proposition.
4Financial Viability
Defines the revenue and cost structures a business needs to meet its operating expenses and financial obligations. Helps entrepreneurs assess how much it costs to deliver the offering and the amount of revenue that can be generated.

Types of Business Models

There are 12 main types of business models. Each type determines how a company makes its profits and who its customers will be.

Model 1
Distributor
Buys products from manufacturers and resells to retailers or the public. Example: UNFI (wholesale food company).
Model 2
Retailer
Sells products directly to the public via store or website. Example: Konga.
Model 3
Franchising
A franchisee buys a license from a franchisor to trade under the franchisor's name. Franchisor provides marketing, operations, and financing support. Franchisee pays royalties based on agreed percentage of sales.
Model 4
Subscription
Charges customers for continuous access to a product or service. Revenue through repeat purchases. Example: Hulu (streaming).
Model 5
Aggregator
Connects vendors and buyers on the same platform; sells services under its own brand. Earns commission on every transaction. Example: Airbnb.
Model 6
Advertising
Revenue from advertising products and services. Provides free content/services, charges sponsors. Example: Google AdWords (cost-per-click/CPA).
Model 7
Data Selling
Generates revenue by selling or licensing high-quality, exclusive information to other parties for advertising and analysis.
Model 8
SaaS
Software as a Service — delivers applications over the Internet on a "pay as you go" basis. Example: Oracle cloud-based applications.
Model 9
Utility/Usage
Charges customers based on how often goods or services are used. Example: mobile phone call minutes, hotel nightly rates, car rentals.
Model 10
Professional
Provides professional services on a time-and-materials contract. Example: consultants, lawyers, accountants (hourly billing); freelance platforms.
Model 11
Licensing
Earns revenue by granting permission to use protected intellectual property (copyrights, patents, trademarks) in exchange for fees. Example: software licenses, app sales to Apple.
Model 12
Freemium
Mixes free basic services with premium upgraded services. Free tier has limits; premium tier offers more storage, functionality, performance. Example: LinkedIn.

Business Model Canvas
Proposed by Alexander Osterwalder (2005), supervised by Yves Pigneur
Definition

The Business Model Canvas is a template for strategic management used to create new business models and document existing ones. It offers a visual chart with elements describing a firm's value proposition, infrastructure, customers, and finances.

Osterwalder's canvas has nine building blocks:

Infrastructure (3 blocks)

1Key Activities
The most important activities in executing a company's value proposition. Example for Bic (pen manufacturer): creating an efficient supply chain to drive down costs.
2Key Resources
Resources necessary to create value for the customer — regarded as assets. Can be human, financial, physical, and intellectual.
3Partner Network
To optimize operations and reduce risks, organisations cultivate buyer-supplier relationships and strategic alliances (joint ventures or alliances between competitors or non-competitors).

Offering

4Value Proposition
What distinguishes the company from its competitors. Provides value through: newness, performance, customization, "getting the job done," design, brand/status, price, cost reduction, risk reduction, accessibility, and convenience/usability.

• Quantitative: Price and efficiency
• Qualitative: Overall customer experience and outcome

Customers (3 blocks)

5Customer Segments
Defines the different groups of people or organizations a company aims to reach and serve.
6Channels
How a company communicates with and reaches its customer segments to deliver its value proposition.
7Customer Relationships
The types of relationships a company establishes with specific customer segments.

Finances (2 blocks)

Block 8
Revenue Streams
The cash a company generates from each customer segment.
Block 9
Cost Structure
All costs incurred to operate the business model.
The 9 Blocks Summary

Infrastructure: Key Activities · Key Resources · Partner Network
Offering: Value Proposition
Customers: Customer Segments · Channels · Customer Relationships
Finances: Revenue Streams · Cost Structure
