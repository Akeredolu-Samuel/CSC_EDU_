Introduction to AI & Machine Learning
Topic 1 — AOC Q1, Q11
AOC Q1 & Q11
Definition — Artificial Intelligence

AI refers to the simulation of human intelligence by machines. UNICEF (2021): "machine-based systems that can, given human-defined objectives, make predictions, recommendations, or decisions that influence real or virtual environments."

Definition — Machine Learning

ML is a subset of AI. Dhanalaxmi (2020): "a subset of AI with the capacity to develop algorithms and statistical models with which computer systems can execute tasks without definitive instructions, but only relying on patterns and inference."

The Hierarchy: AI → ML → Deep Learning

  • AI — broadest concept: goal of making machines intelligent.
  • ML — subset of AI: achieving intelligence by learning from data.
  • Deep Learning — subset of ML: multi-layered neural networks on very large datasets. Powers speech recognition, self-driving cars.
Key Insight

Traditional programming: Rules + Data → Answers.
Machine Learning: Data + Answers → Discovers the Rules itself.

Types of AI

Narrow AI (Weak)
Most common today
Performs one specific task well. E.g., facial recognition, spam filters, Google Translate, chess programs.
General AI (Strong)
Hypothetical
Same cognitive ability as humans across any domain. Does NOT exist yet.
Super AI
Theoretical
Surpasses human intelligence in all areas. Entirely speculative.

Sub-fields of AI

  • Machine Learning — machines learn patterns from data
  • Natural Language Processing (NLP) — computers understand and generate human language
  • Computer Vision — computers interpret visual information
  • Robotics — machines perform physical tasks
  • Expert Systems — simulate decision-making of human experts

Ethics & Societal Implications of AI
Topic 2 — AOC Q2, Q5, Q6
AOC Q2, Q5 & Q6 — High Priority

Key Ethical Concerns

A
Algorithmic Bias
AI learns from historical data that contains human biases. Example: Joy Buolamwini (MIT) found facial recognition is significantly less accurate for darker-skinned women. In education: a model trained on past pass/fail data may disadvantage students from underfunded schools. Bias enters at: (1) data collection, (2) feature selection, (3) model evaluation.
B
Data Privacy
AI requires large amounts of personal data. Student performance data must be protected from misuse and unauthorised access. Balance between data-driven insights and individual privacy is essential.
C
Digital Divide & Inequitable Access
Not all regions have equal access to technology and reliable electricity — especially in Nigeria. As Moemeke (2024) notes: "inequalities in education could be made worse if certain students are unable to access AI-driven resources."
D
Over-Reliance on AI
Students risk weakening critical thinking if AI always provides instant answers. AI should augment, not replace, human thinking.
E
Diminished Human Interaction
AI overuse may undermine human connections essential for social development. The goal is augmentation — AI to assist the teacher, not replace.

4 Negative Societal Consequences + Mitigations (AOC Q6)

ConsequenceMitigation Strategy
Job displacement through automationInvest in AI re-skilling and upskilling programmes
Surveillance & erosion of privacyEnforce strong AI governance frameworks and data protection laws
Spread of disinformation (deepfakes)Develop AI detection tools and media literacy education
Perpetuation of inequality via biased AIMandate diverse, representative training datasets and regular bias audits

Principles of Responsible AI

  • Transparency: Users must understand how AI makes decisions.
  • Fairness: No discrimination based on race, gender, or religion.
  • Accountability: Humans remain responsible for AI consequences.
  • Privacy: AI must comply with data protection standards.
  • Safety: AI must be rigorously tested before deployment.

Supervised vs Unsupervised Learning
Topic 3 — AOC Q4
AOC Q4
FeatureSupervised LearningUnsupervised Learning
Input DataLabelled (input + correct output)Unlabelled (input only)
GoalPredict specific output for new inputsDiscover hidden patterns/groups
Human GuidanceRequires labelled training dataNo labels needed
OutputPrediction (class or number)Clusters or associations
AccuracyGenerally higher (known targets)Lower (no known targets)
Example AlgorithmsLinear Regression, SVM, Decision Tree, Logistic RegressionK-Means, PCA, Apriori
Real-world ExampleSpam detection, price prediction, student pass/failCustomer segmentation, topic discovery

Supervised Learning — Problem Types

  • Classification: Output is a category. E.g., email is spam (1) or not (0); student will pass or fail.
  • Regression: Output is a continuous number. E.g., predicting a student's exam score from study hours.

Common Supervised Learning Algorithms

  • Linear Regression — predicts continuous values
  • Logistic Regression — binary classification (yes/no)
  • Decision Trees — tree-like if-then rules
  • Random Forest — combines many decision trees
  • SVM — finds optimal boundary between classes
  • k-NN — classifies based on most similar training examples
  • Naive Bayes — probabilistic classifier using Bayes' theorem

Unsupervised Learning — Problem Types

  • Clustering: Grouping similar items. E.g., segmenting students by learning style.
  • Dimensionality Reduction: Reducing variables while keeping important info. E.g., PCA.
  • Association: Finding relationship rules. E.g., if milk & bread → also buy eggs.

Reinforcement Learning (RL)

An agent learns by interacting with an environment — receiving rewards for correct actions and penalties for incorrect ones. Like training a dog with treats. Real examples: Google DeepMind AlphaGo, self-driving car training.

Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA)
Topic 5 — AOC Q3
AOC Q3
Definition

EDA is the process of examining and understanding a dataset before building any ML model. It involves summarising characteristics, visualising distributions, identifying relationships, and detecting errors. Core principle: "Garbage in, Garbage out."

Why EDA is Essential

  • Understand the structure and content of the dataset before modelling.
  • Identify and fix missing values, errors, and outliers before they corrupt results.
  • Discover patterns and correlations between variables.
  • Decide which features are most useful for prediction.
  • Choose the most appropriate ML algorithm for the problem.

5 Steps in EDA

1
Load & Inspect
df.head(), df.shape, df.info(), df.describe() — understand rows, columns, data types.
2
Handle Missing Values
Drop rows with too many nulls; fill with mean/median/mode (imputation). df.fillna(df.mean(), inplace=True)
3
Identify & Handle Outliers
Use IQR method or box plots. Outliers distort predictions if not addressed.
4
Visualise the Data
Histograms, box plots, scatter plots, correlation heatmaps, bar charts, pair plots.
5
Feature Engineering
Create new variables, encode categories as numbers, normalise values to the same scale.

Handling Skewed Distributions & Outliers (AOC Q3)

  • Use the Interquartile Range (IQR) method to detect outliers: any value below Q1 − 1.5×IQR or above Q3 + 1.5×IQR is an outlier.
  • For a skewed distribution: apply log transformation or square root to normalize; use median instead of mean as the measure of centre.
  • Visualise with a box plot to spot outliers and understand the spread.
  • Consider winsorisation: cap extreme values at a percentile threshold.

Visualisation Chart Guide

Predictive Analysis & Statistical Models
Topic 6 — AOC Q8

Key Statistical Concepts

ConceptDefinition

VarianceHow spread out values are from the mean.
Standard Deviation√Variance. Low SD = values clustered near mean.
CorrelationLinear relationship between two variables. Range: -1 to +1. Zero = no linear relationship.

Linear Regression

Formula

Y = mX + b   (m = slope, b = y-intercept)
Example: Score = 8.5 × Hours_Studied + 30
Each extra hour of study → +8.5 points predicted score.
Simple: One input variable.  Multiple: Two or more input variables.

Logistic Regression

Despite the name, this is a classification algorithm. Uses the sigmoid function to output a probability between 0 and 1. If probability > 0.5 → class 1; otherwise → class 0.

Key Difference

Linear Regression → predicts a number (e.g. score: 72.4).
Logistic Regression → predicts a class (e.g. Pass or Fail, Yes or No).

Input vs Output Variables (AOC from notes)

  • Input Variables (X / Independent / Features): The data fed INTO the model. E.g., hours studied, attendance rate, previous grade.
  • Output Variables (Y / Dependent / Label/Target): What the model PREDICTS. E.g., exam score, pass/fail result.

Model Evaluation & Performance Metrics
Topic 7 — AOC Q9, Q10
AOC Q9 & Q10 — Very High Priority

The Train/Test Split

Training Set (80%): the model learns from this. Test Set (20%): evaluated on data never seen before. Evaluating on training data gives unrealistically high accuracy — the model memorised, not learned.

Confusion Matrix

Predicted Positive (1)Predicted Negative (0)
Actual Positive (1)True Positive (TP) ✓False Negative (FN) ✗ — missed case
Actual Negative (0)False Positive (FP) ✗ — false alarmTrue Negative (TN) ✓

Key Metrics (AOC Q9)

MetricFormulaWhen to Prioritise
Accuracy(TP + TN) / TotalBalanced classes. Misleading with class imbalance.
PrecisionTP / (TP + FP)When false alarms are costly. E.g., spam filter (important email classified as spam).
Recall (Sensitivity)TP / (TP + FN)When missing positives is costly. E.g., COVID-19 screening (missing infected person).
F1-Score2 × (P × R) / (P + R)When both FP and FN are costly; imbalanced datasets.
ROC-AUCArea under ROC curveOverall model discrimination ability; values closer to 1.0 are better.

Regression Evaluation Metrics

  • MAE (Mean Absolute Error): Average absolute difference between predicted and actual.
  • MSE (Mean Squared Error): Average squared difference. Penalises large errors more.
  • RMSE (Root MSE): √MSE. Same units as output — easier to interpret.
  • R² (R-squared): Proportion of variance explained. 0–1; closer to 1 = better.

Overfitting vs Underfitting (AOC Q10)

K-Fold Cross Validation

The dataset is divided into k equal parts (folds). The model is trained k times, each time using a different fold as the test set. Final metric = average across all runs. More reliable than a single train/test split.

Introduction to Neural Networks
Topic 8 — AOC Q12, Q13

Structure of an Artificial Neural Network

Input Layer
Receives raw data
Each neuron = one feature. 5 input features → 5 input neurons.
Hidden Layers
Computation layers
Extract patterns and representations. Multiple hidden layers = Deep Neural Network.
Output Layer
Final result
Binary classification: 1 neuron. 10-class classification: 10 neurons.

How a Single Neuron Works

1
Receive Inputs (x₁, x₂, x₃…)
2
Multiply by Weights
Each input × its weight (w₁, w₂…) — weights indicate importance.
3
Sum + Bias
z = w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + w₃x₃ + b
4
Apply Activation Function
Introduces non-linearity. Common: ReLU (hidden), Sigmoid (binary output), Softmax (multi-class).
5
Pass Result to Next Layer

Activation Functions

FunctionOutput RangeBest Used For
Sigmoid(0, 1)Binary classification outputs. Suffers vanishing gradient in deep networks.
ReLU[0, ∞)Most common for hidden layers. f(x) = max(0, x). Simple, fast, effective.
SoftmaxProbabilities summing to 1Output layer for multi-class classification problems.

Training: Backpropagation & Gradient Descent

  • Forward pass: data flows through → model makes a prediction.
  • Calculate loss: how wrong was the prediction?
  • Backpropagation: error propagated backwards; each weight's contribution calculated via chain rule.
  • Weight update: weights adjusted by learning rate to reduce error.
  • Repeat for many epochs until the model converges.

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)
Topic 9 — AOC Q12
AOC Q12

CNNs are specialised for data with grid-like topology — primarily images. They have achieved human-level or superhuman performance on many image recognition tasks.

Why Regular ANNs Fail for Images

A 224×224 colour image = 224×224×3 = 150,528 values. Connecting to 1,000 hidden neurons requires over 150 million parameters — computationally expensive and highly prone to overfitting. CNNs solve this through weight sharing and spatial structure.

CNN Architecture (AOC Q12 — Draw & Label)

Architecture Flow

Input Image → [Conv2D + ReLU → MaxPooling] × N → Flatten → Dense (Fully Connected) → Softmax Output

Key Components

Layer TypeRole
Convolutional LayerA filter/kernel slides across the image performing element-wise multiplication to create a feature map. Detects edges, curves, textures → shapes → faces at deeper layers.
Pooling Layer (Max Pooling)Takes the maximum value from a region. Reduces spatial dimensions while keeping key information. Provides translational invariance (recognises features regardless of position).
FlatteningConverts 3D feature maps into a 1D vector for input into Dense layers.
Fully Connected (Dense)Performs high-level reasoning on flattened features.
Softmax OutputConverts raw scores into probabilities summing to 1 for final classification.

CNN Applications

  • Image Classification, Object Detection, Facial Recognition
  • Medical Imaging (detecting cancer in X-rays/MRI)
  • Autonomous Vehicles (detecting pedestrians, signs)
  • OCR — Converting images of text to machine-readable text
Starter Dataset

MNIST — 70,000 images of handwritten digits (0–9). The "Hello World" of deep learning. Freely available in TensorFlow/Keras.

Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) & LSTMs
Topic 10 — AOC Q13
AOC Q13 — High Priority

RNNs are designed for sequential data where order matters. Unlike feedforward networks, RNNs have loops allowing information to be passed from one step to the next — a form of "memory."

Why Sequences Need RNNs

Standard feedforward networks process each input independently with no notion of what came before. RNNs maintain a hidden state updated at each step, carrying context forward through the sequence.

The Vanishing Gradient Problem

Core RNN Limitation

Basic RNNs struggle with long sequences because gradients become extremely small (vanish) during backpropagation. The network effectively forgets events from the distant past.
Example: "I grew up in Lagos and after many years abroad, I still speak..." — the relevant language is connected to "Lagos" mentioned much earlier, which a basic RNN forgets.

LSTM — How it Solves the Problem

Introduced by Hochreiter & Schmidhuber (1997). LSTMs use a cell state and three gating mechanisms:

GateFunction
Forget GateDecides which information from the previous cell state to discard.
Input GateDecides which new information to add to the cell state.
Output GateDecides what to output based on the cell state.

GRU (Gated Recurrent Unit)

A simplified LSTM with fewer parameters. Trains faster, often performs comparably. Combines forget and input gates into a single "update gate." Used when computational efficiency matters.

CNN vs RNN Comparison

FeatureCNNRNN / LSTM
Best ForSpatial data (images, video)Sequential data (text, speech, time series)
Core OperationConvolution with filtersRecurrent computation with hidden state
MemoryNo sequence memoryMaintains memory across time steps
Key StrengthDetecting spatial patternsCapturing temporal dependencies
Key LimitationNot suitable for sequencesBasic RNNs forget long sequences
ApplicationsImage recognition, medical imagingNLP, translation, speech, forecasting

AOC Q15 — Will AI Replace Teachers? (~200 Words)

Essay Framework

The claim that AI will replace teachers is unlikely. While AI provides personalised learning, automated grading, and intelligent tutoring, teachers perform roles far beyond information delivery: mentoring, emotional support, behaviour management, inspiring creativity, and developing social and ethical values — requiring empathy, judgment, and interpersonal skills that current AI cannot replicate.

AI is most effective as a complementary tool: identifying struggling students, recommending materials, and automating administrative tasks — freeing teachers for meaningful human interactions.

Furthermore, AI systems make mistakes, inherit data biases, and lack contextual understanding of individual needs. Human oversight remains essential for fairness, accuracy, and ethical decision-making.

The future of education is therefore a collaboration between teachers and AI — AI will enhance teaching and learning, but human teachers will continue to guide, motivate, and support students throughout their educational journey.

Recommendations for Teachers Using AI

  • Use AI as a complement, not a replacement for teaching.
  • Prioritise activities requiring critical thinking and independence.
  • Ensure equitable access — consider students without reliable internet.
  • Maintain data privacy and transparency about AI tools.
  • Continuously update your own skills as AI evolves rapidly.
