Educational Measurement, Tests & Domains of Learning
Unit 1
Key Distinctions

Measurement — assigning numbers/scores to characteristics (How much?). Assessment — broader gathering of evidence using multiple tools. Evaluation — making a value judgment about results (pass/fail).

Types of Educational Measurement

Norm-Referenced
Student's performance compared to others (normative group). E.g., ranking students 1st to last.
Criterion-Referenced
Performance compared to a fixed standard. E.g., 50% and above = pass.
Ipsative
Performance compared to one's own previous results — assesses individual growth.

Types of Tests

TypeDescription
Aptitude TestMeasures potential/ability to learn. E.g., IQ tests.
Achievement TestMeasures what has been learned. E.g., WAEC, NECO.
Diagnostic TestIdentifies specific areas of difficulty.
Formative TestDuring learning — monitors progress. E.g., class quizzes.
Summative TestEnd of period — evaluates overall achievement. E.g., semester exams.
Standardised TestUniform procedures, consistent administration. E.g., SAT, JAMB.
Teacher-made TestConstructed by classroom teachers for specific content.
Objective TestOne correct answer. E.g., MCQ, true/false, matching.
Essay TestExtended responses; assesses higher-order thinking.
Speed TestEasy items; score depends on number completed in time.
Power TestNo strict time limit; items become progressively harder.
Key Distinction

Aptitude tests predict future performance; Achievement tests measure past/current learning.

Domains of Learning (Bloom)

A. Cognitive Domain (Bloom's Taxonomy)

LevelDescriptionExample Verbs
1. RememberRecall of facts and basic conceptsDefine, List, Name, State
2. UnderstandExplain ideas or conceptsDescribe, Explain, Summarise
3. ApplyUse information in a new situationSolve, Calculate, Demonstrate
4. AnalyseDraw connections among ideasCompare, Differentiate, Examine
5. EvaluateJustify a decision or course of actionJudge, Defend, Criticise
6. CreateProduce new or original workDesign, Construct, Develop

B. Affective Domain — Krathwohl's Taxonomy [RRVOC]

Deals with feelings, emotions, values, and attitudes.

  • Receiving: Being aware/willing to hear. E.g., listening attentively.
  • Responding: Actively participating. E.g., class discussions.
  • Valuing: Attaching worth to an idea. E.g., showing concern for others.
  • Organisation: Prioritising values. E.g., accepting ethical standards.
  • Characterisation: Values guiding behaviour consistently.

C. Psychomotor Domain — Simpson's Taxonomy [PSGMCAO]

Deals with physical movement, coordination, and motor skills.

  • Perception: Using sensory cues to guide motor activity.
  • Set: Readiness to act.
  • Guided Response: Learning through imitation and trial & error.
  • Mechanism: Habitual performance at intermediate skill level.
  • Complex Overt Response: Performing complex movements with expertise.
  • Adaptation: Modifying skills for special requirements.
  • Origination: Creating new movement patterns.

Test Development & Item Analysis
Unit 1 (cont.)

Steps in Test Development

1
Plan the Test
Define purpose, content, and type of items.
2
Prepare a Test Blueprint
A Table of Specifications mapping content to cognitive levels.
3
Write Test Items
Draft questions aligned with the blueprint.
4
Review & Edit Items
Expert review for clarity, correctness, and bias.
5
Conduct Item Analysis
Pilot test + statistical analysis of each item.
6
Finalize the Test
Select best items, arrange, write instructions.
7
Administer the Test
Give test under standardised conditions.
8
Score & Interpret
Apply consistent marking scheme; use scores for decisions.

Item Analysis

A statistical technique used after administration to evaluate the quality of individual test items.

A. Difficulty Index (p)

Formula

p = Number correct ÷ Total number of students
Ideal: 0.30 ≤ p ≤ 0.70. Near 1.0 = very easy; near 0.0 = very difficult.

B. Discrimination Index (D)

Formula

D = (Upper 27% correct – Lower 27% correct) ÷ Number in each group
D > 0.30 = acceptable. D > 0.40 = excellent. Negative D = problematic item.

C. Distractor Analysis

Each wrong option (distractor) should attract some students — especially lower-group students. Non-functional distractors should be replaced.

Validity & Reliability of Instruments
Unit 1 (cont.)

A. Validity

The degree to which a test measures what it is supposed to measure. A test can be reliable but not valid; it cannot be valid without being reliable.

TypeDescription
Content ValidityTest covers all important aspects of the content domain. Assessed via expert judgment and blueprint.
Construct ValidityTest measures the theoretical construct it claims to measure.
Criterion-Related ValidityCorrelation between test scores and an external criterion.
Concurrent ValidityScores correlate with another established measure administered at the same time.
Predictive ValidityTest scores predict future performance on a related measure.
Face ValidityThe test appears (on the surface) to measure what it should — subjective.

B. Reliability

The consistency of a test — it produces similar results under consistent conditions.

MethodDescription
Test-RetestSame test administered to same group on two occasions; scores correlated.
Parallel/Alternate FormsTwo equivalent test versions; scores correlated.
Split-HalfTest divided into two halves (odd/even); halves correlated.
Cronbach AlphaAverage correlation among all items; best for multiple-item tests.
KR-20/KR-21Kuder-Richardson formula for dichotomous (right/wrong) scoring.
Core Principle

Validity is MORE important than reliability — a test that doesn't measure what it should is useless regardless of its consistency.

Research: Meaning, Characteristics & Types
Unit 2
Definition

Research is a formal, systematic, and intensive process of scientific investigation aimed at generating new knowledge.

Characteristics of Research [FLERIISD]

  • Formal: Follows established procedures and standards.
  • Systematic: Follows a logical sequence of steps.
  • Investigative: Seeks answers to specific problems.
  • Intensive: Requires careful, detailed attention.
  • Logical: Based on sound reasoning.
  • Empirical: Based on observable evidence, not just theory.
  • Replicable: Results can be reproduced by other researchers.
  • Deductive.

Types of Research

By Purpose

  • Basic (Pure) Research: Knowledge for its own sake, no immediate practical application.
  • Applied Research: Solves specific practical problems.

By Knowledge Generated

  • Inductive: Specific observations → general conclusions.
  • Deductive: General theory → specific predictions → tested.

By Method

TypeDescriptionFocus
DescriptiveDescribes conditions without manipulationWhat is the current situation?
HistoricalStudies past events using primary & secondary sourcesWhat happened in the past?
ExperimentalManipulates IV to observe effect on DVWhat is the cause?
CorrelationalExamines relationships between variablesAre variables related?
Case StudyIn-depth study of a single unitDeep understanding of a specific case

Types of Variables

Research Questions, Hypotheses & Proposals
Unit 2 (cont.)

Research Questions

Specific questions derived from the research problem that guide the study. They break the broad problem into manageable, investigable parts.

Hypotheses

Definition

A tentative, testable statement conjecturing the expected relationship between variables. An educated guess derived from theory that can be tested statistically.

Characteristics of a Good Hypothesis

  • Stated clearly and unambiguously in declarative form.
  • Conjectures a relationship between two or more variables.
  • Testable with empirical data.
  • Consistent with known facts.
  • Specific — must not use vague adjectives (e.g., "bright students").

Types of Hypotheses

TypeDescription
Null (H₀)States NO significant difference/relationship. Used in statistical testing.
Alternative (H₁/Hₐ)States there IS a significant difference/relationship. Can be directional (one-tailed) or non-directional (two-tailed).
ScientificA suggested solution/intelligent informed guess.
StatisticalA statement about an unknown parameter testable through data.
Important

In educational research, hypotheses are usually stated in null form (H₀) for testing, because statistical tests are designed to reject or fail to reject the null hypothesis.

Research Proposal Structure

  • Title
  • Introduction (Background & Problem Statement)
  • Research Questions & Hypotheses
  • Scope and Limitations
  • Review of Related Literature
  • Theoretical/Conceptual Framework
  • Research Methodology (design, population, sample, instruments, analysis)
  • References
  • Appendices

Research Designs
Unit 3
Definition

A systematic plan/framework a researcher adopts to answer research questions. It defines the type of study, variables, data collection methods, and analysis plan. Think of it as the blueprint of a building.

Experimental Research Designs

Design TypeRandom AssignmentControl GroupStrength
Pre-ExperimentalNoUsually NoWeak
True ExperimentalYesYesStrongest
Quasi-ExperimentalNoUsually YesModerate

Pre-Experimental Designs

  • One-Shot Case Study: Treatment → Observation only. No baseline. [X → O]
  • One-Group Pretest-Posttest: Same group tested before and after. [O₁ → X → O₂]
  • Static Group Comparison: Two existing groups; one receives treatment. [X→O₁ vs O₂]

True Experimental Designs

  • Posttest-Only Control Group: Random assignment; only posttest. [R→X→O₁ vs R→O₂]
  • Pretest-Posttest Control Group: Both groups tested before and after. [R→O₁→X→O₂ vs R→O₃→O₄]
  • Solomon Four-Group: Combines above two designs to control for testing effects.

Quasi-Experimental Designs

  • Non-Equivalent Control Group: Two existing groups; pretest and posttest.
  • One-Group Pretest-Posttest: Single group measured before and after.
  • Interrupted Time-Series: Repeated observations before and after treatment.
Practical Note

True experimental designs require random assignment (gold standard for causality). In schools, random assignment is rarely possible, so quasi-experimental designs are most common in educational research.

Key Symbols

X = Treatment/Intervention  |  O = Observation/Test  |  R = Random Assignment

Other Research Designs

  • Descriptive: Describes existing conditions without manipulation. Sub-types: Survey, Case Study, Document Analysis, Trend Study, Correlational.
  • Historical: Describes and interprets past events using primary (first-hand) and secondary (not first-hand) sources.
  • Correlational: Examines relationships between variables. Ranges from -1 to +1. Correlation ≠ Causation.
  • Causal-Comparative (Ex Post Facto): Examines how an existing past IV affects the DV. No manipulation.

Literature Review, Theoretical & Conceptual Frameworks
Unit 4

Literature review is the systematic study, evaluation, and synthesis of all existing works relevant to the research topic. No meaningful research can be conducted without it.

Purposes of Literature Review

  • Keeps the researcher updated on developments in the field.
  • Identifies gaps in existing knowledge.
  • Helps refine research questions and hypotheses.
  • Provides possible research designs and methodologies.
  • Provides a backdrop for interpreting results.
Important Distinction

Literature review is NOT a list of summaries — it is a critical discussion of what is known, showing areas of agreement, disagreement, and knowledge gaps.

Theoretical vs. Conceptual Framework

FeatureTheoretical FrameworkConceptual Framework
SourceDrawn from existing theories by scholarsDeveloped by the researcher from concepts
ScopeNarrower — focuses on one theoryBroader — covers the whole research project
PurposeProvides theoretical groundingMaps variables and their relationships
RepresentationUsually described in proseOften shown as a diagram or model

Citation & Referencing (APA Style)

  • Paraphrase (end): (Okonkwo & Osundiji, 2003)
  • Paraphrase (start): Okonkwo and Osundiji (2003) note that…
  • Direct quote: According to Osundiji (2006:71), '…'
  • 3+ authors: First citation: Osundiji, Salawu, and Aiyedun (2006); subsequently: Osundiji et al. (2006)

Sample & Sampling Techniques
Unit 5
ConceptDefinition
PopulationAll members of a defined group sharing specific characteristics.
ElementA single member of a given population.
SampleA subset of the population selected for study.
SamplingThe process of selecting a sample from the population.

A. Probability Sampling

Every member has a known, equal chance of selection — allows generalisation.

TypeDescription
Simple RandomEqual chance; lottery, random number tables, or computer-generated numbers.
SystematicSelect every nth element from a list. n = N ÷ sample size.
StratifiedDivide into strata; randomly sample each. Proportionate or Disproportionate.
ClusterNaturally occurring clusters randomly selected; study members within clusters.

B. Non-Probability Sampling

Members do not have equal chances. Cannot be statistically generalised — useful for qualitative/exploratory research.

TypeDescription
Purposive/JudgementalDeliberately selects subjects based on specific traits.
Convenience/AccidentalAny easily available participant. Fast but least representative.
QuotaSubgroups; convenience sampling until each quota is filled.
SnowballInitial participants refer others. Useful for hard-to-reach populations.

Sample Size Formulas

Yamane's Formula

n = N ÷ (1 + Ne²), where N = population size, e = margin of error (usually 0.05).
Rule of thumb: at least 10% of population is generally acceptable.

Data Processing, Analysis & Descriptive Statistics
Unit 7

Data Processing Steps

  • Editing: Check for completeness, legibility, and consistency.
  • Coding: Assign numbers/symbols. E.g., Male=1, Female=2.
  • Classification: Group data into meaningful categories.
  • Tabulation: Organise into frequency tables and cross-tabulations.
  • Data Cleaning: Fix errors, duplicates, missing values, outliers.
GIGO Principle

Garbage In, Garbage Out — inaccurate or poorly processed data produces meaningless or misleading results.

Measures of Central Tendency

MeasureMeaning & When to Use
MeanSum ÷ N. Best for interval/ratio data with no extreme outliers.
MedianMiddle value when ordered. Best when data has extreme values or is skewed.
ModeMost frequent value. Best for nominal data.

Measures of Dispersion

MeasureDescription
RangeMaximum − Minimum. Simple but affected by extreme values.
VarianceAverage of squared deviations from the mean.
Standard Deviation (SD)√Variance. Average distance of each score from the mean. Smaller SD = more clustered.

Computing SD — 5 Steps

1
Calculate the Mean
2
Subtract the Mean from each score
(X − Mean)
3
Square each deviation
(X − Mean)²
4
Calculate Variance
Σ(X−Mean)² ÷ (N−1)
5
Take the Square Root
√Variance = Standard Deviation

ToolWhat it DoesWhen to Use

t-test (Paired)Compares means of same group at 2 time pointsPre-test vs post-test

Hypothesis Testing & Error Types
Unit 7 (cont.)
Definition

Hypothesis testing is a statistical procedure used to determine whether there is enough evidence to reject the null hypothesis (H₀) in favour of the alternative (H₁).

Critical Values & Significance Level (α)

The significance level (α) is the probability of making a Type I error. Common values: α = 0.05 (5%) or α = 0.01 (1%).

  • If the computed test statistic exceeds the critical value → reject H₀.
  • If it does not exceed → fail to reject H₀.

Types of Errors in Hypothesis Testing

Error TypeDecisionRealityConsequence
Type I Error (α)Reject H₀H₀ is trueFalse positive — conclude effect exists when it doesn't.
Type II Error (β)Fail to reject H₀H₀ is falseFalse negative — miss a real effect.

Qualitative Data Analysis

MethodDescription
Thematic AnalysisIdentifying recurring patterns (themes) across a dataset.
Content AnalysisSystematic coding of text into categories to count/quantify themes.
Grounded TheoryTheory emerges from data through iterative coding.
Narrative AnalysisAnalysing the structure and content of personal stories.
Discourse AnalysisExamining how language constructs meaning in context.
Descriptive vs. Inferential

Descriptive: Summarises and describes the data you have — does not generalise beyond the data.
Inferential: Uses sample data to make predictions or generalise to a larger population.

Research Ethics

  • Obtain informed consent from participants.
  • Ensure confidentiality and anonymity of data.
  • Do no harm (physical, psychological, social).
  • Report findings honestly without fabrication or falsification.
  • Acknowledge all sources (avoid plagiarism).
  • Obtain necessary institutional approvals.
