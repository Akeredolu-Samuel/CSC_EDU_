Key Terms – E-Learning & E-Moderator
Objective 1

1.1 What is E-Learning?

E-learning (electronic learning) refers to the acquisition of knowledge and skills through electronic technologies and media. It encompasses any form of learning that is enabled electronically, from simple online resources that supplement face-to-face teaching, to fully online courses with no physical interaction.

Scholarly Definitions

Guri-Rosenbilt (2005): E-learning uses electronic media for various learning purposes, from classroom add-on functions to complete substitutions for face-to-face meetings.

Clark and Mayer (2016): E-learning is instruction delivered through digital devices to support learning.

eLearningNC.gov: Learning by utilising electronic technologies to access educational curricula outside traditional classrooms.

E-Learning Core Concept

The use of electronic technologies to deliver, support, and enhance teaching, learning, and assessment. It may be fully online or blend with face-to-face elements.

1.2 What is an E-Moderator?

An e-moderator is a facilitator of online learning who guides and supports learners in virtual environments. The term was popularised by Professor Gilly Salmon in her seminal book E-Moderating: The Key to Teaching and Learning Online (2001).

E-Moderator Definition

A person who maintains an online learning environment for the benefit of participants. They facilitate communication, guide discussions, motivate learners, and promote knowledge construction in digital spaces.

According to IGI Global, the e-moderator:

  • Maintains the online learning environment for the benefit of all participants.
  • Is familiar with the capabilities of the learning environment.
  • May have additional privileges (e.g., loading learning materials, creating/moderating forums, enrolling students).
  • Represents the learning community with leadership qualities.
  • Is responsible for motivating and supervising interventions by community members.

Gilly Salmon's definition (2011): The essential role of the e-moderator is promoting human interaction and communication through the modelling, conveying, and building of knowledge and skills. This reflects a constructivist approach (learners build knowledge through engagement, not passive reception).

E-Moderation (also called tele-tutoring) means filling an educational role in a digital environment. This includes university teaching, corporate training, workshops, and seminars.

The E-Learning Environment
Objective 2

2.1 Characteristics of the E-Learning Environment

The e-learning environment is the digital space where teaching and learning occur. It can be fully online or blended (a combination of online and face-to-face).

  • Technology-mediated: All communication and content delivery happens through digital tools (LMS platforms, video conferencing, discussion forums, etc.).
  • Flexible access: Learners can access content at any time and from any location with internet access.
  • Learner-centred: The environment shifts focus from teacher-led instruction to student-driven exploration.
  • Interactive: Forums, chats, quizzes, peer feedback, and collaborative tools allow for rich interaction.
  • Asynchronous and/or synchronous: Learners may interact in real-time or at different times.
  • Scalable: Can accommodate small groups or thousands of learners simultaneously.

2.2 Common E-Learning Platforms (LMS)

A Learning Management System (LMS) is software that houses course content, manages learner enrolment, tracks progress, and enables communication.

Canvas
Widely used in higher education; user-friendly interface.
Google Classroom
Free, integrates with Google Workspace tools.
Moodle
Open-source; highly customisable; popular globally.
Schoology
Combines LMS features with social networking.
Blackboard
Enterprise-level LMS common in universities.

2.3 Types of E-Learning Environments

  • Fully Online: All instruction, content, and assessment delivered digitally. No face-to-face meetings.
  • Blended/Hybrid: Combines online components with face-to-face sessions. Offers flexibility while retaining human contact.
  • Synchronous Online: Students and instructor meet at the same time via video conferencing or live chat.
  • Asynchronous Online: Students engage with materials and discussions at their own pace, within given deadlines.

Skills & Responsibilities of an E-Moderator
Objective 3

3.1 Overview

The e-moderator wears many hats: teacher, facilitator, motivator, community builder, and technical guide. According to Nicky Hockly's e-Moderation Station, the key skills can be grouped into the Top Ten Moderator Skills.

3.2 The Top Ten E-Moderator Skills (Hockly, 2010)

1. PLN
Personal Learning Network
Develop and maintain a professional learning network (blogs, X/Twitter, webinars) to stay updated. Ongoing, not a one-time event.
2. Enthusiasm
Genuine Interest
Uninspired teachers produce uninspired learners. Variety in tasks, time allocation, reflection, and experimentation are required.
3. Communication
Textual Intuition
Skilled at 'reading between the lines', detecting tone, emotion, confusion, or disengagement. Careful word choice is essential.
4. Presence
Visible Yet Supportive
Present without dominating. Knowing when to intervene (guide) and when to step back to allow peer interaction and independent learning.
5. Develop Discussions
Dialogue Building
Ask powerful questions, encourage critical thinking, and build on existing threads. Weaving and summarising are key techniques.
6. Scaffolding
Calibrated Support
Provide the right level of support—not too much (kills challenge) and not too little (creates frustration). Adjust support over time.
7. Empathy
Student Perspective
See the process from the learner's view. Taking an online course as a student is the best way to build this skill.
8. Sensitivity
Diversity Awareness
Adapt to diverse backgrounds and learning styles. Use pre-course questionnaires and respect cultural norms.
9. Socialising
Community Cultivation
Actively combat isolation through cafe-style posts, peer introductions, and social tasks. Enhances retention and satisfaction.
10. Cycles
Lifecycles Planning
Plan for each phase of the course lifecycle: warm welcome/orientation (start), active tasks (middle), and closure/achievement (end).

3.3 Core Responsibilities of an E-Moderator

  • Setting up and managing the online learning environment.
  • Welcoming and orienting new learners.
  • Facilitating discussions and motivating participation.
  • Providing timely and constructive feedback.
  • Monitoring learner progress and addressing difficulties.
  • Maintaining academic standards and community guidelines.
  • Summarising and weaving discussion threads.
  • Supporting learners with technical issues.
  • Encouraging peer collaboration and knowledge construction.

E-Tivities – Purpose, Design & Community
Objective 4

4.1 What is an E-Tivity?

The term 'e-tivity' was coined by Professor Gilly Salmon. It refers to a framework for active, interactive online learning activities designed and led by an e-moderator.

E-Tivity Core Concept

A motivating, engaging, and purposeful online activity designed and led by an e-moderator. It involves a stimulus (spark), an individual or group task, interaction among participants (responses), and a summary or feedback from the e-moderator (plenary).

4.2 Key Features of E-Tivities (Salmon, 2002)

  • Motivating, engaging, and purposeful.
  • Based on interaction between learners mainly through written messages/contributions.
  • Designed and led by an e-moderator.
  • Asynchronous (primarily) — though synchronous variants exist.
  • Inexpensive and easy to run (usually through forums, online bulletin boards).
  • Require an online posting or contribution from each participant.
  • Include an interactive or participative element (responding to others' posts).
  • End with a summary, feedback, or critique from the e-moderator (the 'plenary').

4.3 Purpose of E-Tivities

  • Offer students a clear and effective framework for achieving learning outcomes.
  • Create opportunities for active participation beyond passive content consumption.
  • Build community through shared tasks and peer responses.
  • Bridge Salmon's five stages of online learning with specific activities at each stage.
  • Make online engagement purposeful, not tokenistic.

4.4 E-Tivity Design Elements

1
Title
A catchy, memorable name.
2
Purpose
Clear learning outcome or objective.
3
Spark
A resource, question, or challenge to stimulate thought.
4
Task
What the learner must contribute (post reflection, response).
5
Response
How learners interact (e.g. reply to at least two peers).
6
Schedule
Timeline for when to post and respond.
7
Plenary
The moderator's summary, weaving, or feedback at the end.

4.5 E-Tivities & Community of Learning

E-tivities are central to building a community of learning (a group of learners) who collectively construct knowledge through shared experience and dialogue. When e-tivities are well-designed:

  • Learners feel connected to peers despite physical distance.
  • Knowledge is co-constructed through dialogue, not just transmitted by the instructor.
  • Learners take increasing responsibility for their own and each other's learning.
  • The e-moderator moves from direct guide to facilitator and observer.

Gilly Salmon's Five-Stage Model
Objective 5

Gilly Salmon's Five-Stage Model is a framework for understanding how online learners progress in their engagement with e-learning. It is rooted in social constructivism. The model provides a scaffold for both course design and e-moderator interventions.

1
Stage 1 – Access and Motivation
Focus: Getting learners onto the platform. Activity: Welcoming, guiding, providing technical instructions, troubleshooting access issues. E-tivity Example: 'Post a brief message to confirm you can access the forum.'
2
Stage 2 – Online Socialisation
Focus: Building an online identity and trust. Activity: Create social, low-stakes activities, model friendly communication norms, validate participation. E-tivity Example: 'Introduce yourself and share one thing you hope to learn. Reply to two peers.'
3
Stage 3 – Information Exchange
Focus: Sharing information, resources, and ideas. Activity: Provide content, facilitate sharing, model how to synthesise resources, step back slightly. E-tivity Example: 'Research an online tool. Share a summary and one strength/weakness. Respond to a classmate.'
4
Stage 4 – Knowledge Construction
Focus: Active discussion, debate, and collaboration. Activity: Weave threads, link/synthesise contributions, pose challenging questions, highlight consensus/divergence. E-tivity Example: 'Given the reading on digital equity, do you believe online learning increases or reduces educational inequality? Argue your position.'
5
Stage 5 – Development
Focus: Self-directed learning, reflection, and real application. Activity: Encourage reflection, support application of concepts, create closure and achievement. E-tivity Example: 'Design a short e-tivity for your own teaching context. Reflect on how Salmon's model informed your design.'

5.3 Summary Table

Stage Name Learner Activity E-Moderator Role
1. Access & Motivation Access the platform; build motivation Welcome, guide, encourage, troubleshoot
2. Online Socialisation Build identity; interact with peers Model norms; create social tasks
3. Information Exchange Share resources and ideas Provide content; facilitate sharing
4. Knowledge Construction Debate, critique, collaborate Weave threads; pose challenges
5. Development Reflect; apply learning; self-direct Encourage reflection; provide closure

Synchronous vs. Asynchronous E-Tivities
Objective 6

6.1 Synchronous Learning

Synchronous e-learning occurs in real time (all participants are online) at the same time.

Examples: Live video lectures (Zoom, Teams, Meet), live webinars, real-time group chats, live polls, virtual office hours.

Advantages: Immediate feedback, real-time peer collaboration, stronger social presence/community.

Disadvantages: Low scheduling flexibility, difficult across time zones, technical disruptions disrupt the whole group.

6.2 Asynchronous Learning

Asynchronous e-learning does not require participants to be online at the same time. Learners engage with materials at their own pace.

Examples: Pre-recorded video lectures, discussion forums, emails, downloadable PDFs, podcasts.

Advantages: High flexibility, allows more reflection time, global accessibility, thoughtful communication.

Disadvantages: Can feel isolated, requires high self-discipline, delayed feedback, risk of 'lurking'.

6.3 Comparison Table

Feature Synchronous E-Tivity Asynchronous E-Tivity
Timing All at the same time Participants' own schedule
Interaction Real-time; instant responses Delayed; reflective responses
Tools used Zoom, Teams, live chat Forums, email, recorded video
Best for Discussions, Q&A, debates Reflections, research tasks
Flexibility Low — fixed schedule High — within deadlines
Community feel Stronger real-time bonding Built over time through posts

Moderating Live (Synchronous) Environments
Objective 7

7.1 Practical Strategies for Live Online Moderation

Moderating a live online session requires managing technology, people, time, and content simultaneously.

Before the session:

  • Clearly define the moderator's role and communicate expectations to all participants.
  • Prepare 'house rules' for online conduct and share them in advance.
  • Test all technology and have a backup plan for failures.
  • Paste tech support contacts into the chat area at the start.
  • Prepare an agenda and share it with participants.

During the session:

  • Have the moderator introduce themselves clearly when the session begins.
  • Use the moderator to relay in-room proceedings to the online audience (for hybrid settings).
  • Actively involve online participants — acknowledge questions, reactions, and chat messages.
  • Manage time carefully — keep the session on track.
  • Use polls, breakout rooms, and shared documents to maintain engagement.
  • Monitor chat actively for questions, technical issues, or off-topic content.
  • Summarise key points periodically to keep all participants aligned.

After the session:

  • Post a summary of key discussion points in the LMS.
  • Follow up on unanswered questions.
  • Gather feedback from participants.
  • Share recordings if the session was recorded.

7.2 Techniques for Engagement in Live Environments

  • Weaving: Explicitly connecting contributions from different participants to show coherence.
  • Questioning: Posing thought-provoking questions that require more than a yes/no answer.
  • Summarising: Periodically recapping key ideas to reinforce learning.
  • Directing: Inviting quieter participants to share views.
  • Stepping back: Allowing learner-to-learner dialogue without constant moderator interruption.

Issues in Online & Blended Environments
Objective 8

8.1 Common Challenges & Solutions

  • 1. Technical Problems: Connectivity issues, software incompatibility, device limitations.
    Solution: Clear technical guides, support channels, and backup plans.
  • 2. Student Isolation and Disengagement: feeling anonymous, leading to 'lurking'.
    Solution: Strong community building at Stages 1-2, check-ins, collaborative tasks.
  • 3. Online Flaming: Hostile, aggressive, or offensive communication.
    Solution: Early netiquette rules; address incidents promptly and privately.
  • 4. Assessment and Academic Integrity: Concerns about verification and plagiarism.
    Solution: Vary assessments (reflections, discussion, project), proctoring, AI detectors.
  • 5. Balancing Sync and Async: Coherence between in-person and online parts.
    Solution: Link online tasks directly to physical classes; use forums to prepare for sync meets.
  • 6. Digital Literacy Gaps: Learners uncomfortable with technology.
    Solution: Orientation sessions, multiple content formats, supportive environment.
  • 7. Time Management and Procrastination: Flexibility leads to falling behind.
    Solution: Clear deadlines, weekly check-ins, structured posting windows.
  • 8. Participation Inequality: Some dominate, others remain silent.
    Solution: Directed questions, design e-tivities requiring individual posts before peer responses.
What is Flaming?

Online Flaming refers to hostile, aggressive, or offensive online communication. Without face-to-face cues, tone can be misread and conflict can escalate quickly in text spaces.

Drafting a Course Design for an Online Space
Objective 9

9.1 Key Principles of Online Course Design

A well-designed online course is built around clear learning objectives, scaffolded activities, and meaningful interaction. Key principles include:

  • Alignment: Objectives, activities, and assessments must align.
  • Scaffolding: Build content and complexity progressively (mirror Salmon's five stages).
  • Variety: Mix content types (video, text, audio) and activity formats.
  • Interaction: Design for learner-content, learner-learner, and learner-instructor interaction.
  • Accessibility: Ensure all learners can engage with materials.
  • Assessment: Use formative and summative assessments appropriately.

9.2 Steps in Blended/Online Course Design

Step 1 – Define Learning Outcomes: What should learners know, understand, or be able to do by the end?

Step 2 – Map Content to Stages: Align content and activities with Salmon's five-stage model to scaffold learning.

Step 3 – Select the Platform: Choose an appropriate LMS (Canvas, Moodle, Google Classroom, etc.) based on institutional context.

Step 4 – Design E-Tivities: Create purposeful, interactive activities for each module/week. Include spark, task, response, and plenary.

Step 5 – Design Assessment: Plan formative assessments (discussion posts, quizzes) and summative assessments (projects, essays).

Step 6 – Build and Organise Content: Structure the LMS logically week-by-week or module-by-module. Include clear navigation.

Step 7 – Pilot and Refine: Test the course with a small group before full launch. Gather feedback and revise.

Emerging Trends in E-Moderating
Objective 10

10.1 Artificial Intelligence (AI) in E-Learning

AI is transforming e-learning and the role of the e-moderator through:

  • Personalised learning: AI adapts content, pace, and difficulty in real time.
  • Automated assessment: instant feedback and identification of knowledge gaps.
  • Predictive analytics: alerts e-moderators to at-risk learners early.
  • AI tutors and chatbots: on-demand support outside of moderated hours.
  • Administrative support: automates grading, scheduling, and admin tasks.

Implication: As AI handles routine tasks, the e-moderator's uniquely human skills—empathy, community building, nuanced feedback, and critical dialogue facilitation—become even more valuable.

10.2 Other Key Trends

  • Microlearning: Content in small, focused bursts (3-10 minutes) like short clips, infographics, and quick quizzes.
  • Mobile Learning (m-Learning): Mobile-first designs so content is responsive on small screens.
  • Gamification: Points, badges, leaderboards, and challenges to increase engagement and retention.
  • AR & VR: Immersive learning. VR-trained employees are 4x faster to train and 275% more confident.
  • Data Analytics: LMS analytics tracking student behaviour. Learners in the lowest performance quartile interact 45% less.
  • Social Platforms: Social-media-like dynamics. remote education shows a 30% engagement increase.
  • Blended & Hybrid Norms: Post-pandemic norm. Fully online delivery is an complementary, student-centric option.

10.9 Implications for the E-Moderator Role

Emerging trends mean the e-moderator must stay continuously updated (PLN skill is critical), be comfortable with data tools/LMS analytics, design for mobile/microlearning, collaborate with AI, navigate immersive environments, and advocate for digital equity.
